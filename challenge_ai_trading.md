## Big Question

**Can an AI agent be trusted with money?**

AI must be used with particular caution in high-risk, high-stakes environments, where one incorrect recommendation can seriously affect people’s safety, opportunities, finances, or privacy. Consider an AI system driving a car, piloting an airplane, allocating resources on a power grid, helping diagnose cancer, evaluating a loan or college application, screening job candidates, or powering an app that handles banking details, personal information, or Social Security numbers. In settings like these, an answer that merely sounds convincing is not enough: its evidence must be checked, its limitations understood, and a human must remain accountable for the decision.

Most of these settings are difficult to recreate in a classroom because we are not pilots, power-grid managers, doctors, bankers, admissions officers, or hiring managers. Financial decision-making, however, gives us an accessible way to examine many of the same questions about risk, trust, evidence, uncertainty, and human responsibility. We will therefore use a **simulated** trading environment to explore high-stakes AI decision-making without risking real money or private financial information.

In this challenge, you will use an AI assistant to help generate simulated stock-trading recommendations. Then you will place simulated trades in a trading simulator and reflect on your reasoning, decisions, and results. Personal notes can help, but a journal is optional.

The goal is **not** to become a stock trader.

The goal is to learn how AI can help with decision-making, why AI can be risky, and why humans must still check, question, and take responsibility for AI-generated recommendations.

## Step 1: Create an AI Trading Assistant Prompt

You will use an AI tool as a structured assistant.

You may use:

- ChatGPT
- Claude
- Gemini
- Microsoft Copilot
- VS Code with an AI assistant
- Another instructor-approved AI tool

Your AI assistant should not simply answer, “Buy this stock” or “Sell this stock.” Your next-door neighbor Joe can do that.

Instead, it should produce a structured recommendation with evidence, risks, uncertainty, and reasons it could be wrong.

---

## Starter Prompt

You may start from "Time to buy MSFT now?"

Or you may copy, paste, and modify this prompt:

```text
You are a cautious educational stock-analysis assistant helping me with a classroom simulation.

For the ticker I provide, create a structured memo with the following sections:

1. Ticker
2. Company or fund summary
3. Recent price movement
4. Three possible bullish arguments
5. Three possible bearish arguments
6. Important risks or upcoming events
7. What information may be missing
8. Simulated trade suggestion: buy, sell, or hold
9. Confidence level from 1 to 5
10. Reasons this recommendation could be wrong

Do not claim certainty.
Do not give real-money financial advice.
This is only for a classroom simulation.
Clearly separate facts from predictions.
```

## AI Risk Audit Questions

For each AI recommendation, answer these questions:

1. Did the AI provide specific evidence?
2. Could you check the evidence?
3. Did the AI mention uncertainty?
4. Did the AI explain why it might be wrong?
5. Did the AI sound too confident?
6. Did the AI ignore any major risks?
7. Did the AI use vague language?
8. Did the AI make predictions that sounded like facts?
9. Did the AI recommend action without enough support?
10. Did you feel tempted to trust the AI because it sounded professional?

---

## Common AI Risks to Watch For

| Risk | What It Might Look Like |
|---|---|
| Hallucination | The AI invents facts, numbers, news, or events |
| Outdated information | The AI misses recent changes |
| Overconfidence | The AI says a stock “will” go up or down |
| Weak evidence | The AI gives vague reasons without support |
| Recency bias | The AI focuses too much on one recent headline |
| Hype chasing | The AI recommends a popular stock without careful reasoning |
| Missing risk | The AI ignores competition, earnings, valuation, or market conditions |
| Automation bias | You trust the AI because it sounds professional |
| Poor prompt design | Your prompt pushes the AI toward a confident answer |
| No accountability | The AI gives advice, but a human must deal with the consequences |

### Reflection Report Format

Write a **two-page AI Trading Agent Reflection Report** using the following format:

- **Page size:** US Letter (8.5 × 11 inches)
- **Font:** 12-point Times New Roman
- **Margins:** 1 inch on all sides
- **Line spacing:** Single-spaced within each paragraph
- **Paragraph spacing:** Leave one blank line between paragraphs; do not add extra blank lines
- **Alignment:** Left-aligned
- **Section headings:** Use the numbered headings below in 12-point bold text
- **Title page:** Do not include a separate title page
- **File format:** Submit as a PDF unless your instructor specifies otherwise

The report should be two pages of written content and should include the following sections.

---

### 1. My AI Trading Assistant

Describe the AI tool and prompt you used.

For example:

- Which AI tool you used
- Your prompt
- Whether you modified the starter prompt
- What your AI assistant was supposed to do

---

### 2. My Simulated Trades

Summarize your simulated trades. Don't give me the whole lists, just highlight the important ones or your general strategy/theme.

For example:

- Tickers
- Dates
- Buy/sell/hold decisions
- Simulated entry prices
- Later outcomes

---

### 3. My Trading Decisions and Reasoning

Explain the thinking behind your decisions. You may draw on personal notes if you kept them, but a journal is not required.

For example, explain:

- Why you made some trade
- Whether you followed or rejected the AI’s recommendation
- What risks you noticed
- What information you thought was missing

---

### 4. Best AI Recommendation

Choose the AI recommendation that seemed most useful.

Explain:

- What made it useful?
- Did it include evidence?
- Did it include risks?
- Was it appropriately cautious?
- Did the outcome match the reasoning?

---

### 5. Weakest or Most Questionable AI Recommendation

Choose the AI recommendation that seemed weakest or most questionable.

Explain:

- What was wrong or missing?
- Did the AI hallucinate or make unsupported claims?
- Did it sound too confident?
- Did it ignore uncertainty?
- Would you trust this recommendation with real money?

---

### 6. Human Responsibility

Explain your role as the human decision-maker.

Answer:

- Did you follow the AI?
- Did you override the AI?
- Did you check the AI’s claims?
- Who would be responsible if this were real money?
- What should a responsible human do before acting on AI advice?

---

### 7. Final Reflection

Answer this question:

> After this activity, would you trust an AI agent to make financial decisions for you? Why or why not?

Your answer should be thoughtful and specific. Use examples from your own simulated trades and decisions.

## Selection for the Final Discussion

We will review the trading records and reflection report together and select the strongest submissions for our final discussion, with permission to share. We will consider:

- **Clear records:** Complete, readable simulator results explained in the reflection report.

Simulated performance is part of the story, but the highest return alone does not make the strongest submission. A losing trade with careful reasoning and an insightful reflection can be especially useful for discussion. When comparing results, we will consider the simulation dates, starting balance, and trading rules.

## What Makes a Strong Submission?

A strong submission:

- Uses the AI assistant in a structured way
- Clearly explains trading decisions in the reflection report
- Uses the simulator correctly
- Explains the reasoning behind each trade
- Identifies risks and uncertainty
- Questions the AI instead of blindly trusting it
- Reflects on human responsibility

---

## What Makes a Weak Submission?

A weak submission:

- Only reports whether money was gained or lost
- Does not explain the thinking behind trading decisions
- Does not explain the AI prompt
- Blindly follows the AI
- Ignores risks
- Treats the simulation like real financial advice
- Focuses only on winning instead of learning

This challenge is not about proving that AI can beat the market.

The most important skill is not trading.
