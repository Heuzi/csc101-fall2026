## Big Question

**Can an AI agent be trusted with money?**

AI must be used with particular caution in high-risk, high-stakes environments, where one incorrect recommendation can seriously affect people’s safety, opportunities, finances, or privacy. Consider an AI system driving a car, piloting an airplane, allocating resources on a power grid, helping diagnose cancer, evaluating a loan or college application, screening job candidates, or powering an app that handles banking details, personal information, or Social Security numbers. In settings like these, an answer that merely sounds convincing is not enough: its evidence must be checked, its limitations understood, and a human must remain accountable for the decision.

Most of these settings are difficult to recreate in a classroom because we are not pilots, power-grid managers, doctors, bankers, admissions officers, or hiring managers. Financial decision-making, however, gives us an accessible way to examine many of the same questions about risk, trust, evidence, uncertainty, and human responsibility. We will therefore use a **simulated** trading environment to explore high-stakes AI decision-making without risking real money or private financial information.

In this challenge, you will use an AI assistant to help generate simulated stock-trading recommendations. Then you will place simulated trades in a trading simulator and keep your own personal notes about your reasoning, decisions, and reflections.

The goal is **not** to become a stock trader.

The goal is to learn how AI can help with decision-making, why AI can be risky, and why humans must still check, question, and take responsibility for AI-generated recommendations.

You will use an AI tool as a structured assistant.

You may use:

- Microsoft Copilot

---

## Starter Prompt

You may copy, paste, and modify this prompt:

```text
You are a cautious educational stock-analysis assistant helping me with a classroom simulation.

For the ticker I provide, create a structured memo with the following sections:

1. Ticker
2. Company or fund summary
3. Recent price movement
4. Three possible bullish arguments
5. Three possible bearish arguments
6. Important risks or upcoming events
7. What information may be missing
8. Simulated trade suggestion: buy, sell, or hold
9. Confidence level from 1 to 5
10. Reasons this recommendation could be wrong

Do not claim certainty.
Do not give real-money financial advice.
This is only for a classroom simulation.
Clearly separate facts from predictions.
```

## Step 4: Make Your Own Human Decision

You do not have to follow the AI.

After reading the AI’s recommendation, make your own decision:

- Buy
- Sell
- Hold
- Do nothing

Before placing the simulated trade, write a short note explaining your decision.

Your note should answer:

1. What did the AI recommend?
2. Do I agree with the AI?
3. What evidence seems strongest?
4. What evidence seems weakest?
5. What could go wrong?
6. Am I trusting the AI too much?

## Step 6: Track the Outcome

After some time has passed, check what happened.

For each simulated trade, record:

| Item | What to Record |
|---|---|
| Current price | Price when you checked later |
| Gain/loss | Whether the simulated trade went up or down |
| AI accuracy | Did the AI’s prediction seem correct, incorrect, or unclear? |
| Process quality | Was the AI’s reasoning good, even if the result was unlucky? |
| Reflection | What did you learn? |

Remember:

A profitable trade does not prove the AI was smart.

An unprofitable trade does not automatically prove the AI was bad.

You are evaluating the **quality of the reasoning**, not just the outcome.

## AI Risk Audit Questions

For each AI recommendation, answer these questions:

1. Did the AI provide specific evidence?
2. Could you check the evidence?
3. Did the AI mention uncertainty?
4. Did the AI explain why it might be wrong?
5. Did the AI sound too confident?
6. Did the AI ignore any major risks?
7. Did the AI use vague language?
8. Did the AI make predictions that sounded like facts?
9. Did the AI recommend action without enough support?
10. Did you feel tempted to trust the AI because it sounded professional?

---

## Common AI Risks to Watch For

| Risk | What It Might Look Like |
|---|---|
| Hallucination | The AI invents facts, numbers, news, or events |
| Outdated information | The AI misses recent changes |
| Overconfidence | The AI says a stock “will” go up or down |
| Weak evidence | The AI gives vague reasons without support |
| Recency bias | The AI focuses too much on one recent headline |
| Hype chasing | The AI recommends a popular stock without careful reasoning |
| Missing risk | The AI ignores competition, earnings, valuation, or market conditions |
| Automation bias | You trust the AI because it sounds professional |
| Poor prompt design | Your prompt pushes the AI toward a confident answer |
| No accountability | The AI gives advice, but a human must deal with the consequences |

## What Makes a Weak Submission?

A weak submission:

- Only reports whether money was gained or lost

- Does not explain the AI prompt
- Blindly follows the AI
- Ignores risks
